chop

maximilian becht · final project year 1

This project investigates how collectively reappropriated, low-tech systems can serve as tools for democratic resource management and community self-determination. In response to increasing social and ecological fragmentation driven by centralized and growth-dependent infrastructures, it seeks to develop and document technologies that enable small-scale autonomy, collective maintenance, and knowledge sharing.

Drawing from degrowth, the project rejects the notion that progress must be tied to increased complexity, wealth, and consumption. Instead, it employs simplicity, sustainability and local adaptability as design values. Technologies are treated not as neutral tools, but as political objects that shape relations — either reinforcing dependence on corporate systems, or enabling dynamic forms of cooperation. By treating resources such as water, tools and knowledge as commons — collectively appropriated and regulated — the project aligns with historical and contemporary practices that resist privatization. It views autonomy not as isolation, but as interdependence structured by mutual trust and shared responsibility.

Conviviality, as defined by Ivan Illich, informs the project's approach to tool design: tools should be understandable, modifiable, and usable by the communities they serve. A convivial tool expands the capacity for action without creating new dependencies. The project aims to explore how such tools can benefit knowledge exchange, situated expertise, and community maintenance, rather than outsourcing those capacities to proprietary systems.

The project focuses on low-complexity, upcycled, and context-aware tools — not because they are nostalgic, but because they are maintainable. These tools support hacking as a democratic act — the practice of reclaiming agency over systems otherwise tied to growth-dependent infrastructures.

Ultimately, the project is about interpendence with the goal to nurture a locally distributed capacity for decision-making, adaptation, and care. Taking part in the production of the resources we use — understanding their flow, where they come from, and what it takes to acquire them — helps cultivate a grounded and responsible relationship with the world around us. If we the heat we use comes from a flame we maintain, when water flows through a system we understand, when our food is cooked on a stove we built brick by brick, those resources become tangible. They stop feeling infinite or abstract. This tangibility invites restraint, not as a form of denial, but as an act of care. It gives us a nudge to go from passive consumption to active participation. It becomes harder to waste what you’ve worked to produce, and easier to share what you value. Making resources visible and manageable at a human scale is a key strategy in resisting the alienation and excess that industrial systems normalize. Through self-sufficiency, the goal is to find a sense of shared stewardship — a feeling that these systems are ours to sustain, and that how we use them shapes the communities we live in.
